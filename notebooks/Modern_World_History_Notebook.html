2The big picture

Between about 1300 and 1600, educated Europeans, starting in the cities of northern Italy, changed how they looked at human life. They returned to Greek and Roman texts, valued human ability and life in this world, and made art and writing that studied nature closely. This shift is called the Renaissance, French for "rebirth". It did not overthrow the medieval world at once. It grew out of a change in the economy: trade had made Italian towns rich, and rich townsmen paid for scholars and artists who served their own tastes and needs. Printing then carried the new learning across Europe, and that wider reading public prepared the ground for the Reformation (W02), modern science and the voyages of exploration.

3Core notes

W01.1 The medieval background and why change came

Medieval Western Europe rested on two institutions. Feudalism tied the peasant to a lord's land and tied the lord to a king through military service. The Roman Catholic Church owned vast lands, controlled education and gave the main explanation of life: this world was a preparation for the next, and truth lay in scripture as explained by the clergy. Scholasticism, the learning of the medieval universities, used logic mainly to support that teaching.

From about the 11th century this order began to loosen. Farm output rose, towns grew, and the Crusades widened trade with the eastern Mediterranean. Then the Black Death (1347–51) killed perhaps a third of the population. Labour became scarce, so surviving peasants could demand better terms and serfdom weakened in the west. The Church's prestige also fell, partly because of the plague and partly because of the Great Schism (1378–1417), when rival popes claimed office. A society with more money, more towns and less certainty was ready for new ideas.

W01.2 Why Italy came first

The question "why Italy?" is the core of the RAS 2016 question. Five factors worked together.

  • Trade and wealth. Venice and Genoa dominated Mediterranean trade with the East. Florence grew rich on woollen cloth and banking.
  • Independent city-states. Italy had no single monarch. Florence, Venice and Milan governed themselves or were ruled by local families, so civic life and public service were valued. A merchant or lawyer needed rhetoric, history and ethics more than theology.
  • Patrons. The Medici bankers of Florence, the Sforza dukes of Milan and the popes at Rome competed for prestige by paying artists and collecting manuscripts.
  • The Roman inheritance. Ruins, statues and inscriptions stood all around Italians, who saw ancient Rome as their own past.
  • Contact with Greek and Arab learning. Greek scholars came from Byzantium: Chrysoloras taught at Florence from 1397, and more arrived around the fall of Constantinople in 1453. Works of Aristotle had already reached Europe through Arabic translations and the commentaries of Ibn Rushd (Averroes), and medical knowledge through Ibn Sina (Avicenna).

Humanism. Humanism was first an education, the studia humanitatis: grammar, rhetoric, history, poetry and moral philosophy, studied from classical texts. It arose because the urban elite wanted a training for public and business life that the Church-centred schools did not give. It worked through careful reading of original texts. Lorenzo Valla used language study to prove in 1440 that the Donation of Constantine, a document that supported papal claims to territory, was a later forgery. Humanists did not usually reject God; Erasmus was a devout Christian. What changed was the emphasis, from the next world to human dignity and ability in this one. Pico della Mirandola's Oration on the Dignity of Man (1486) is the classic statement.

W01.3 Art: seeing the world as it is

Medieval art served religious teaching and did not try to show space or bodies exactly. Renaissance artists wanted their paintings to look true, and to do that they studied mathematics and anatomy. Linear perspective, worked out by Brunelleschi in the early 15th century, showed depth on a flat surface. Chiaroscuro used light and shade to model forms. Patrons wanted their fame and their city displayed, so portraits and secular subjects grew alongside religious ones.

Leonardo shows the Renaissance ideal of the many-sided person: his notebooks hold anatomy, machines and studies of water alongside painting. The artist's status rose too, from a craftsman in a guild to a celebrated individual. Giorgio Vasari's Lives of the Artists (1550) recorded this, and used the word rinascita (rebirth).

W01.4 Literature and political thought

Writing in the vernacular widened the reading public beyond those who knew Latin. Dante's Divine Comedy, Petrarch's sonnets and Boccaccio's Decameron made Tuscan the base of literary Italian. Later, national literatures grew elsewhere: Chaucer in English, Rabelais and Montaigne in French, Cervantes (Don Quixote) in Spanish, and Shakespeare.

The most lasting political book was Machiavelli's The Prince. Italy was being invaded by France and Spain from 1494, and Machiavelli, a Florentine official, asked how a ruler actually keeps power. He judged actions by results and not by Christian morality, which separated politics from religious ethics. This is why he is seen as a founder of modern political thought. Castiglione's The Courtier (1528) set the model of the cultivated gentleman.

W01.5 Printing, science and exploration

Printing made the Renaissance a European event. Paper had come from China through the Arab world. Johannes Gutenberg combined movable metal type, oil-based ink and a press at Mainz, and printed his Bible about 1455. Books became cheap and identical copies spread quickly, so scholars could compare and correct texts and ideas could not easily be suppressed. Luther's writings of 1517 and after spread by this route (W02).

Science began to rest on observation. Copernicus argued in 1543 that the earth moves around the sun, against the Ptolemaic view the Church accepted. Vesalius corrected ancient anatomy from dissection in the same year. Galileo, later, used the telescope and was forced by the Inquisition to recant in 1633. This line of work led to the Scientific Revolution and to Newton (W02).

Exploration had practical causes. Europeans wanted direct access to Asian spices and gold, and Ottoman control of the eastern routes raised the incentive, though Portugal's Atlantic voyages had begun well before 1453. The compass, better maps and the caravel made long voyages possible. Dias rounded the Cape in 1488, Columbus reached the Americas in 1492, Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498, and Magellan's expedition (1519–22) sailed round the world. This opened the colonial age that reached India (I26).

W01.6 How far did it spread, and whom did it reach?

North of the Alps the Renaissance took a more religious form. Christian humanists such as Erasmus and More used classical scholarship to criticise Church abuses and return to the Bible itself; Erasmus published a Greek New Testament in 1516. Dürer took Italian technique to Germany. Because these scholars read scripture directly and printed widely, the Northern Renaissance led straight to the Reformation.

The limits matter for a balanced answer. The movement touched a small literate urban elite, and most peasants saw little change. Renaissance states were not more peaceful, and belief in witchcraft and persecution continued. Women's educational gains were confined to a few, such as the writer Christine de Pizan and the patron Isabella d'Este. Its results were long-term: a confidence in reason and in the individual, and a habit of testing old authority against evidence.

Was it really a "rebirth"? Jacob Burckhardt (1860) described a sharp break, the birth of modern individualism in Italy. C.H. Haskins (1927) replied that a "Renaissance of the twelfth century" had already revived Latin classics and universities. Joan Kelly (1977) asked whether women had a Renaissance at all. Others stress the debt to Arab and Byzantine scholars, which shows the classical revival was not a purely European achievement. A safe answer: real change, but gradual, elite and built on medieval foundations.

4What historians say

Jacob Burckhardt, The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy (1860), set the classic view of individualism born in Italy. Jules Michelet had already popularised the term in 1855. C.H. Haskins pushed the roots back to the 12th century. Peter Burke treats it as a social process in which patrons, markets and audiences matter as much as genius. NCERT's Themes in World History also stresses the Arab contribution and the role of Italian towns.

Exam traps

  • The Last Supper is a wall painting in Milan, not in Florence or Rome. The Mona Lisa is the painting in the Louvre.
  • The fall of Constantinople (1453) did not cause the Renaissance. Petrarch died in 1374 and Greek teaching at Florence began in 1397.
  • The Sistine Chapel is Michelangelo, not Leonardo; The School of Athens is Raphael.
  • Humanists were not atheists. Humanism changed the focus of study, and most humanists remained Christian.
  • Printing paper came from China through the Arab world; Gutenberg did not invent paper.

  • Political: city-states and patrons
  • Intellectual: classical and Arab learning, weak Church
  • Printing spreads it
  • Conclude: gradual process
Model answer (106 words)

The Renaissance was the revival of classical learning and a new human-centred outlook, from about the 14th century. Its base was economic: Mediterranean trade made Italian towns rich and created a lay urban class. Independent city-states such as Florence and Venice valued civic skills, and patrons like the Medici funded scholars and artists. Roman ruins, Greek scholars from Byzantium and Arabic translations of Aristotle supplied the learning. The Black Death and the Great Schism weakened feudal and Church authority. Gutenberg's printing press then spread these ideas across Europe. Thus the Renaissance grew from social and economic change rather than a sudden break with the medieval past.

2The big picture

In 1517 a German monk, Martin Luther, attacked the Church's sale of indulgences. Within a generation Western Christendom had split into Catholic and Protestant churches, and rulers had taken new power over religion. Luther himself wanted to reform the Church and not to divide it. His challenge spread because it joined three forces: long anger at Church wealth and abuses, German princes who wanted power and land, and a printing press that made pamphlets cheap. The Catholic Church reformed itself in reply, and over a century of religious wars ended in a Europe of sovereign states. Then, from the late 17th century, thinkers of the Enlightenment applied the methods of the new science to society itself, and asked whether kings, churches and privileges could justify themselves before reason. Those questions led to the revolutions of America and France.

3Core notes

W02.1 Why the Reformation came

By 1500 the Church was the richest landowner in Europe and it collected tithes and fees from all. Many of its offices were bought and sold (simony), bishops often held several posts at once, and clergy were exempt from ordinary courts. Earlier critics had raised these issues: John Wycliffe in England and Jan Hus in Bohemia, who was burnt in 1415. Humanists such as Erasmus mocked abuses and sent readers back to the Greek New Testament.

Politics made the difference. Rising monarchs and German princes resented money flowing to Rome and courts beyond their control. Germany was especially open: it had no strong central ruler, and hundreds of small states could protect a rebel. The trigger came when Pope Leo X needed money to rebuild St Peter's in Rome, and indulgences were sold in Germany by the Dominican Johann Tetzel.

Indulgences. The Church taught that sins carried punishment after death, which could be reduced by good works. An indulgence granted a reduction drawn from the merits of Christ and the saints. It arose as a pious practice, for example as a reward for crusading, but by 1500 it had become a source of revenue, and preachers made it sound as if a payment bought salvation. Luther's protest was theological: no payment could affect God's forgiveness. The Council of Trent abolished the office of indulgence seller in 1562.

W02.2 Luther and his ideas

Luther, a professor at Wittenberg, reached his central belief through personal anxiety: humans cannot earn salvation by their own works; it comes only through faith, as God's free gift. In 1517 he set out 95 Theses against indulgences. Printers spread them through Germany within weeks. In 1520 he published three major pamphlets, and in 1521 he refused to recant before Emperor Charles V at the Diet of Worms. Frederick the Wise of Saxony sheltered him at Wartburg, where he translated the New Testament into German.

Universal priesthood (priesthood of all believers). Luther taught that every baptised Christian has direct access to God and needs no priest as a go-between. He stated it in his 1520 address to the German nobility, in order to deny the clergy's claim to be a separate and higher estate. In practice clergy remained, but as ministers appointed for a duty and not as a sacred caste. They could marry, and the laity were encouraged to read the Bible in their own language. It also let secular rulers act in church matters.

Luther was socially conservative. When peasants rose in the Peasants' War (1524–25), citing his language of Christian freedom, he urged princes to crush them. Lutheranism therefore grew as a religion protected by princes. The name "Protestant" comes from the princes' protest at the Diet of Speyer (1529). The Peace of Augsburg (1555) let each German ruler choose Catholicism or Lutheranism for his subjects: cuius regio, eius religio.

W02.3 Other reformers and the Catholic reply

Zwingli led reform in Zurich. John Calvin, a French lawyer, made Geneva a disciplined godly city from 1541. His predestination taught that God has already chosen who will be saved. Calvinism had a clear organisation without bishops, and it spread to Scotland (John Knox), the Netherlands, France (the Huguenots) and England (the Puritans). In England the cause was political: Henry VIII wanted his marriage annulled, the pope refused, and the Act of Supremacy (1534) made the king head of the church.

The Catholic response is called the Counter-Reformation or Catholic Reformation. The Council of Trent (1545–63) reaffirmed doctrine, including the seven sacraments and the value of works, but it also corrected abuses and ordered seminaries to train priests. The Jesuits, founded by Ignatius Loyola and approved in 1540, ran excellent schools and sent missionaries abroad; Francis Xavier reached Goa in 1542. The Roman Inquisition and the Index of Prohibited Books policed belief.

W02.4 What the Reformation changed

Religious unity ended, and war followed: the French Wars of Religion, the Dutch Revolt and the Thirty Years War (1618–48). The Peace of Westphalia (1648) recognised rulers' authority within their territories, and it is often taken as the start of the modern system of sovereign states. Monarchs gained church land and control over religion. Bible translation strengthened national languages. Literacy grew because believers were expected to read.

Did Protestantism create capitalism? Max Weber, in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905), argued that Calvinist believers sought signs of being chosen through disciplined work and saving, which encouraged capitalist habits. R.H. Tawney saw a more complicated two-way relation, and critics point to capitalism in Catholic Italy and Flanders long before Calvin. A careful answer treats it as a helpful influence and not as a single cause.

W02.5 From the Scientific Revolution to the Enlightenment

The new science gave Europe a method and a model. Copernicus, Kepler and Galileo overturned the old picture of the heavens. Francis Bacon urged experiment and induction, and Descartes urged systematic doubt. Newton's Principia (1687) showed that one set of mathematical laws governed falling apples and planets. If nature had laws that reason could find, thinkers asked, might society have them too? Religious wars and the absolute monarchy of Louis XIV made that question urgent, and the growth of print, salons and coffee houses gave it a public.

The Enlightenment. The Enlightenment was the 18th-century European intellectual movement that held that reason, not tradition or revelation, should judge institutions and guide human progress. It grew from the success of science and from disgust at religious persecution and arbitrary rule. It worked through books, the Encyclopédie, salons and correspondence, with the French philosophes at its centre. Kant summed it up in 1784 with the motto "Dare to know". Its critics, from Romantics onward, said that it undervalued feeling, custom and faith.

Some rulers, called enlightened despots, took up parts of the programme: Frederick II of Prussia, Catherine II of Russia and Joseph II of Austria. They reformed law and administration but kept their own power. The sharper results came elsewhere: Locke shaped the American Declaration of Independence (W04), and Rousseau shaped the language of the French Revolution (W05). Indian reformers such as Rammohun Roy later read these writers too (I35).

4What historians say

Max Weber linked Calvinism and capitalism, and R.H. Tawney qualified the claim in Religion and the Rise of Capitalism (1926). Peter Gay treats the Enlightenment as a confident movement of secular reason, while Robert Darnton studies its books, publishers and readers, and Roger Chartier asks whether it caused the French Revolution or was invented afterwards as its cause. Horkheimer and Adorno criticise it for treating reason as a tool of domination.

5Fact bank

  • Luther set out his 95 Theses at Wittenberg in 1517.
  • Johann Tetzel sold the indulgence that provoked Luther; the money was linked to rebuilding St Peter's.
  • Luther refused to recant at the Diet of Worms in 1521.
  • Universal priesthood means all baptised believers have direct access to God without a priest.
  • The word Protestant comes from the protest at the Diet of Speyer in 1529.
  • The Peace of Augsburg (1555) set the rule cuius regio, eius religio.
  • Calvin taught predestination and made Geneva his centre; he wrote the Institutes.
  • The Act of Supremacy (1534) made Henry VIII head of the Church of England.
  • The Council of Trent sat from 1545 to 1563.
  • Ignatius Loyola founded the Society of Jesus, approved in 1540.
  • The Peace of Westphalia (1648) ended the Thirty Years War.
  • Newton published the Principia in 1687.
  • Montesquieu proposed the separation of powers in The Spirit of the Laws (1748).
  • Rousseau wrote The Social Contract (1762), with the idea of the general will.
  • Kant gave the Enlightenment the motto "Dare to know" in 1784.

Exam traps

  • Predestination is Calvin, not Luther. Luther's core idea is justification by faith.
  • Luther did not set out to found a new church, and he opposed the rebel peasants of 1524–25.
  • The English break of 1534 was political, not doctrinal, in its origin.
  • Separation of powers is Montesquieu; the general will is Rousseau; natural rights and consent are Locke.
  • The Enlightenment is 18th century; do not date it with the Renaissance.

10 marks · 100 words Examine the causes and consequences of the Reformation in Europe.

How to frame it

  • Abuses and earlier critics
  • Rulers and German politics
  • Printing and the trigger of indulgences
  • Consequences: division, Trent, wars
  • State power, literacy
  • Conclusion
Model answer (96 words)

The Reformation arose from Church wealth, simony and clerical privilege, already criticised by Wycliffe, Hus and Erasmus. Princes resented payments to Rome, and fragmented Germany could shelter a rebel. The sale of indulgences provoked Luther's 95 Theses (1517), which printing spread quickly. Western Christianity then split into Lutheran, Calvinist, Anglican and Catholic churches. Rome replied with the Council of Trent and the Jesuits. Religious wars ended at Westphalia (1648) in a system of sovereign states. Rulers gained church lands and control, while vernacular Bibles strengthened literacy and national languages. A religious protest thus reshaped European politics.

5 marks · 50 words How did the Enlightenment prepare the ground for the revolutions of the late 18th century?

How to frame it

  • Reason against authority
  • Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau
  • Applied in America and France
Model answer (52 words)

The Enlightenment made reason the test of authority. Locke's natural rights and consent, Montesquieu's separation of powers and Rousseau's general will offered alternatives to absolute rule. America's Declaration and Constitution used Locke and Montesquieu; France's Declaration of Rights used Rousseau. Ideas alone did not cause revolution, but they gave grievances a language.

8One-screen revision card

  • Causes: abuses, wealth, princes against Rome, humanism, printing; trigger: indulgences (Tetzel), 1517.
  • Luther: faith alone, scripture alone, universal priesthood; Worms 1521; opposed peasants 1525.
  • Calvin: predestination, Geneva. England: Act of Supremacy 1534. Augsburg 1555: cuius regio, eius religio.
  • Counter-Reformation: Trent 1545–63, Jesuits 1540, Inquisition, Index. Westphalia 1648: sovereign states.
  • Enlightenment: reason as the test. Locke (rights), Montesquieu (separation), Voltaire (toleration), Rousseau (general will), Smith (markets).

9Self-test

  1. What provoked the 95 Theses?

    Answer

    The sale of indulgences in Germany by Tetzel, linked to the rebuilding of St Peter's.

  2. Define universal priesthood.

    Answer

    All baptised believers have direct access to God and need no priest as an intermediary.

  3. What rule did the Peace of Augsburg set?

    Answer

    Each ruler decides the religion of his territory: cuius regio, eius religio.

  4. What was the Council of Trent?

    Answer

    The Catholic council of 1545–63 that reaffirmed doctrine, corrected abuses and ordered seminaries.

  5. Who wrote The Spirit of the Laws?

    Answer

    Montesquieu, in 1748.

  6. Why did Luther survive when Hus had been burnt?

    Answer

    Printing spread his ideas quickly, and German princes such as Frederick the Wise protected him for their own political reasons.

  7. How did universal priesthood affect the power of secular rulers?

    Answer

    By denying that clergy were a separate estate, it let rulers act in church matters.

  8. Why did the new science lead to the Enlightenment?

    Answer

    Newton's success suggested that reason could also find laws of society and test its institutions.

  9. Connect: how does the Weber thesis link W02 and W03?

    Answer

    Weber argued Calvinist discipline and saving encouraged capitalist habits, which some see as part of the background to industrial growth.

  10. Compare universal priesthood with Bhakti ideas in India.

    Answer

    Both rejected priests as necessary intermediaries and valued the believer's direct relationship with God, and both used vernacular languages.
